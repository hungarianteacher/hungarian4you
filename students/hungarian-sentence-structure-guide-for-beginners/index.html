A1 · Ultimate Guide

The logic behind
Hungarian sentences.

Hungarian looks scrambled from the outside. It isn't. Once you see the one trick behind it, you can start building your own sentences today — no memorising word order required.

English The boy sees a dog order = meaning
Hungarian A fiú lát egy kutyát ending = meaning
01 · The big idea

Endings do the job that word order does in English

This single fact explains almost everything confusing about Hungarian sentences. Once it clicks, the rest of this guide is just details.

In English, you know who is doing what because of where the words stand. "The dog sees the boy" and "The boy sees the dog" mean opposite things — only the order changed.

In Hungarian, the noun that receives the action gets a little tag glued onto its end — usually -t. That tag says "I'm the object" no matter where the word stands in the sentence. So word order becomes free to do a different job: showing emphasis, not grammar.

A kutya lát egy macskát.
The dog sees a cat.
Egy macskát lát a kutya.
It's a cat that the dog sees. (same core meaning, cat now emphasised)
subject verb object ending / tag

Remember this one line

English: word position carries meaning. Hungarian: word endings carry meaning, so position is free.

The five parts of a Hungarian sentence

Hungarian grammar doesn't slice a sentence up quite the way English does. Instead of "subject / verb / object / adverb / adjective", it names five parts, and one of them — the predicate — works a little differently than the English "verb" does.

HungarianClosest English ideaNotes
AlanySubjectstraightforward — who or what the sentence is about
ÁllítmányPredicateusually the verb, but can be an adjective when there's no verb at all (see Section 08)
TárgyDirect objectonly the direct object — this is the -t word from Section 05
HatározóAdverb / indirect objectwhere, when, how, with whom — a broad basket, often one of those noun+ending combos from Section 02
JelzőAdjectiveonly when it sits directly in front of a noun, describing it

Here's one sentence with every part labelled, the way a Hungarian grammar book would break it down:

Péter minden reggel visz egy piros almát az iskolába.
Péter takes a red apple to school every morning.
Word(s)Hungarian partRole
PéterAlanysubject
viszÁllítmánypredicate (here, a verb: "takes")
almátTárgydirect object
reggelHatározóadverb of time

What to actually remember from this table: a Jelző always stands right in front of the word it describes, so its position is never a puzzle. A Határozó ("adverb") can wander fairly freely. The Alany and the Tárgy tend to sit close to the Állítmány. And the Állítmány — the predicate — is the anchor everything else is arranged around, which is exactly what the next few sections are about.
02 · How words grow

Hungarian words snap together like train cars

Instead of separate little words (in, on, to, with...), Hungarian glues an ending directly onto the noun. One root, one or more endings clicked on the end.

Why this matters for you

English prepositions (in, on, into, with, at) sit before the noun. Hungarian endings sit after the noun, stuck right onto it. Same job, opposite side. Once you know a handful of endings, you can place things "in", "on", "into" or "with" anything in your vocabulary.

Good news: you already do this in English with just one ending — the plural "-s" (dog → dogs). Hungarian simply has more endings, and uses them more often, instead of separate little words.
03 · One quirk to expect

Endings change their vowel to match the word

You met -ban and -ben above — same job, different vowel. That's not random. Hungarian words come in two "sound families", and the ending picks the version that matches.

Back vowel words (a, á, o, ó, u, ú)

  • ház (house) →házban
  • iskola (school) → iskolában
  • asztal (table) → asztalon
  • kutya (dog) → kutyával

Front vowel words (e, é, i, í, ö, ő, ü, ű)

  • víz (water) → vízben
  • könyv (book) → könyvben
  • kert (garden) → kertben
  • szem (eye) → szemmel
Beginner shortcut: don't calculate this — just listen and repeat. Your ear will learn the pattern faster than the rule will. For now, when you meet a new word, learn it together with one ending (like "házban" as a pair), not the bare root alone.
04 · Fewer decisions

No gender, and only two "the"s

Some of the hardest parts of other languages simply don't exist in Hungarian. This is where it gives you a break.

WordUsed forExample
a"the", before a consonant sounda kutya (the dog)
az"the", before a vowel soundaz alma (the apple)
egy"a / an" (also literally "one")egy alma (an apple)

There's no masculine, feminine, or neuter — nouns don't change form for gender at all. Even better: ő means both "he" and "she". You never have to guess or choose.

What this means for you

Every noun you learn is grammatically "neutral". You never need to remember "is it der, die or das" the way German makes you. One less thing to memorise, every single time.

05 · The most useful ending

Marking the object: add -t

This is the ending that makes free word order possible, so it's worth mastering early. Whatever receives the action of the verb gets -t glued onto the end.

Root+ endingObject formMeaning
kutya+ tkutyátdog → the dog (as object)
alma+ talmátapple → the apple (as object)
víz+ etvizetwater → the water (as object)
könyv+ etkönyvetbook → the book (as object)
ház+ atházathouse → the house (as object)
Pattern to notice: words ending in a vowel usually just add -t (sometimes lengthening the last vowel: a→á). Words ending in a consonant usually need a small connecting vowel first: -et / -at / -ot. Note also that "víz" shortens its long í to a plain i once an ending is attached: víz → vizet, not "vízet".
Én iszom vizet.
I drink water.
Péter eszik egy almát.
Péter eats an apple.
One thing we're skipping on purpose: Hungarian verbs also change slightly depending on whether the object is "definite" (the/this) or "indefinite" (a/some) — that's called definite conjugation, and it's your next lesson after this one. To keep things simple here, every example in this guide uses an indefinite object.
06 · Using the freedom

Whatever you emphasise goes right before the verb

The verb (Állítmány) is the anchor of a Hungarian sentence. The spot right in front of it is the "spotlight" — and any part of the sentence can stand there, not just the object.

Neutral, safe order for beginners: Subject → Verb → Object. This works every time and sounds completely natural.

Péter visz egy piros almát az iskolába.
Péter takes a red apple to school. (plain statement, no special emphasis)

Now watch what happens as we move a different word into the spot right before "visz" (takes). Each version answers a slightly different implied question.

Egy piros almát visz Péter az iskolába.
It's a red apple that Péter takes to school. (emphasis on the object — not a sandwich, an apple)
Péter visz egy piros almát az iskolába.
It's Péter who takes a red apple to school. (emphasis on the subject — not his sister)
Az iskolába visz Péter egy piros almát.
It's to school that Péter takes the apple. (emphasis on the "Határozó" — not to work)
The pattern, generalised: Alany, Tárgy, Határozó — any of them can take the emphasis spot right before the Állítmány. Hungarian doesn't reserve that position for one specific job; it reserves it for whatever the speaker wants to highlight.
Beginner rule of thumb: until this feels natural, always default to Subject → Verb → Object. You'll be understood every time. Save reordering for when you want to sound emphatic — it's a bonus, not a requirement.
07 · A bonus shortcut

The verb ending already says who — so pronouns can hide

Just like endings replace prepositions, they also replace the need for a subject pronoun. Every Hungarian verb ending already announces "I", "you", "we"...

esz
em
=
eszem
esz- (eat) + em (the "I" ending) → eszem already means "I eat", no "én" needed.
Eszem egy almát.
I'm eating an apple. ("Én" is optional here — the ending already tells you who.)
When do Hungarians keep "én", "te"...? Mostly to add emphasis — "I ate it (not you)". As a beginner, it's completely fine to keep the pronoun every time — it's never wrong, just sometimes a little extra.
08 · One irregular but common verb

"Van" (to be) — used, and sometimes skipped

Van/vagyok/vagy... behaves normally when it means location or existence, but it disappears in one very specific spot: describing a quality with "he/she/it/they" in the present tense.

A könyv az asztalon van.
The book is on the table. (location → "van" stays)
A ház szép.
The house is beautiful. (quality, "he/she/it" form → "van" disappears! Not "A ház szép van".)
Én boldog vagyok.
I am happy. ("I / you" forms always keep vagyok / vagy, even with a quality.)
Quick rule: only the "he / she / it / they" forms (van / vannak) get dropped, and only next to an adjective. Every other use of "to be" behaves exactly as you'd expect.
Terminology note: in Hungarian grammar, "szép" here isn't treated as an object — it's still the Állítmány (predicate) of the sentence, just a névszói állítmány ("nominal predicate") rather than a verbal one. That's exactly why "van" can vanish: the predicate doesn't need a verb to do its job.
09 · Saying no & asking

Negation and questions, briefly

Negation: put "nem" right before the word you're negating

Én nem iszom kávét.
I don't drink coffee.

"nem" usually sits right before the verb, but if you want to negate a specific word instead, put "nem" right before that word — the spotlight rule from Section 06 applies to "no" too.

Yes/no questions: same words, rising tone (or add -e)

Szereted a kávét?
Do you like coffee? (same word order as a statement — only the tone/question mark changes)

Open questions: the question word jumps to the front

WordMeaningWordMeaning
ki?who?hol?where?
mi?what?mikor?when?
Mi van az asztalon?
What is on the table?

13 · Keep this nearby

The whole logic, in ten lines

  1. Endings, not order, show who does what to whom.
  2. Prepositions become endings glued to the noun: házban, not "in ház".
  3. Endings shift vowels to match the word: -ban vs -ben, -ba vs -be, and so on.
  4. No gender, ever. ő = he or she, no guessing.
  5. Only two "the"s: a (before consonants), az (before vowels).
  6. The object of a sentence gets -t (with a linking vowel if needed: -at/-et/-ot).
  7. Safe default order: Subject → Verb → Object.
  8. The verb (Állítmány) is the anchor — whatever you emphasise (subject, object, or adverb) moves to right before it.
  9. The verb ending already shows the subject — pronouns are often optional.
  10. nem goes right before whatever you're negating.
